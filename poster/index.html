Care Flow: A Bilingual Digital Queue Management System
for Outpatient Services at AIC Kijabe Hospital

Care Flow Development Team

AIC Kijabe Hospital, Kijabe, Kenya

Introduction

The outpatient department at AIC Kijabe Hospital managed daily patient flow through manual, paper-based queues. Overcrowded waiting areas produced long and unpredictable waits, queue-jumping disputes, and missed calls, and staff had no auditable record of the patient journey from arrival to consultation.

English-only signage and announcements excluded Kiswahili-speaking patients, while cash-only payment steps fragmented the visit record: registration, payment and clinical care were documented in separate, disconnected paper trails.

Patient waiting time is a documented determinant of satisfaction with outpatient care (Oche & Adamu, 2013). The World Health Organization recommends digital health interventions for health-system strengthening (WHO, 2019), and Kenya’s National eHealth Policy 2016–2030 calls for people-centred digital health services. These priorities motivate a locally designed, bilingual digital queue for outpatient care.

Objective To design, build and deploy a secure, bilingual electronic queue system covering the full outpatient journey — registration → payment → triage → consultation — with live room displays and voice announcements.

Methods

  • Setting & design. AIC Kijabe Hospital outpatient department; iterative design–build–deploy cycles during 2026; registration-first patient flow.
  • Queue engine. PostgreSQL security-definer daily sequences issue atomic token numbers — duplicate tokens are impossible by construction; tokens reset daily.
  • Payments. M-Pesa Daraja STK push to the patient’s phone, with insurance-provider verification as an alternative path; payment confirmed before triage.
  • Patient flow. Bilingual self-check-in kiosk (English / Kiswahili; new-patient check-in is Kiswahili-first) → triage → waiting → room call.
  • Calling. Live display board covering 57 active consultation rooms; public-address announcements generated with Gemini text-to-speech (3 voices, double chime + token read twice, with browser-speech fallback).
  • Staff tools. Role-based dashboards, biometric work-ID sign-in (WebAuthn), room assignments, attendance and summons.
  • Security. 12 layered controls including Cloudflare Turnstile, database-backed rate limiting (5 attempts / 10 min → 15 min block), row-level security, and an append-only audit trail.
  • Data lifecycle. Nightly 00:00 (Africa/Nairobi) archive of completed visits to Excel (.xlsx) workbooks auto-delivered to the superadmin’s device; patients still waiting or in rooms are never purged.
  • Stack. React 19 + TanStack Start + TypeScript; PostgreSQL on Neon; hosted on Render; installable offline-capable PWA.

Results

The complete system is deployed and running end to end in production. Queue integrity is enforced at the database level; every completed visit is archived nightly and auto-exported to Excel. Formal waiting-time and satisfaction outcomes are the planned next phase of work.

Conclusions

A low-cost, locally built digital queue system is feasible for a Kenyan mission hospital using standard web technologies. Atomic tokens make the queue fair by construction; Kiswahili-first check-in makes it language-inclusive; M-Pesa integration keeps payment inside the visit record; and nightly Excel archives make the data auditable.

Limitations. Development took place at a single site, and waiting-time and patient-satisfaction outcomes have not yet been measured.

Next steps. The next phase is a prospective before–after waiting-time study with a patient-satisfaction survey, followed by scale-up to partner facilities.
